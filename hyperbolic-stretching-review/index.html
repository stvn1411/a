What is Hyperbolic Stretching and how is it meant to work?

Hyperbolic Stretching is a flexibility program from Adam Kubinyi, and the current version is presented as the FlexTense Method. The maker says it is used by more than 500,000 people, including beginners, older adults and athletes. That is the maker's own figure and we could not verify it independently.

The method rests on a contract-relax idea. You press the limb gently against a firm surface to create tension, then relax and move deeper into the stretch. The maker explains this through the "inverse stretch reflex" and says it lets you gain flexibility with less force and shorter sessions. Routines last about 8 minutes, and the program includes morning and evening flows, routines for hamstrings, hips, splits, forward folds, back bends and the upper body, and short maintenance flows to keep your flexibility once you have reached your goal.

Because it is a video program, you can follow along on any device. The sales page says no equipment or partner is needed, and its FAQ addresses older adults and people who prefer not to go down on the floor.

We did not take the program ourselves, so we judge it by the official page and by the research on stretching.

Does Hyperbolic Stretching work?

We'd put it this way: the evidence behind regular stretching is solid, and contract-relax style stretching is one of the better-performing techniques.

  • A meta-analysis of 77 studies found that stretch training increases joint range of motion and that PNF and static stretching worked better than ballistic or dynamic stretching for long-term gains. Women showed larger gains than men on average.[1]
  • A review of 65 studies found that stretching produces a moderate increase in stretch tolerance and a small decrease in stiffness, without a measurable change in muscle fascicle length.[2]

That second point is worth knowing. The maker explains the program's results through a reflex that relaxes the muscle, whereas researchers currently think most flexibility gains come from your nervous system allowing a bigger stretch. Either way, the practical takeaway is the same: regular, relaxed stretching increases range of motion, and a program that makes it easy to be consistent is helpful.

What this means for you: expect your range of motion to improve with regular practice, and keep it up with the maintenance flows. The program has not had its own clinical trial, so treat the sales page's "at any age" and speed claims as the maker's own, and progress at the pace that feels comfortable.

How to get the most out of Hyperbolic Stretching

  1. Start with the beginner routine for the area you want to loosen, such as hamstrings or hips.
  2. Practice a little every day. Eight minutes is easier to keep up than an hour once a week.
  3. Keep the tension gentle. Contract-relax stretching should feel firm, not painful. Never force a stretch.
  4. Use the morning or evening flows to loosen up for the day or wind down before bed.
  5. Switch to the maintenance flows once you reach your goal, repeated once or twice a week.
  6. Warm up first and use the dynamic routines before sport or training.

Is Hyperbolic Stretching safe? Side effects

Stretching is gentle for most people, but pushing too hard can cause strains, and the contract-relax approach involves deliberate muscle tension.

  • Check with your doctor first if you have a recent injury, joint or spine condition, osteoporosis, or have recently had surgery.
  • Stretch to a firm but comfortable level. Stop if you feel sharp pain, numbness or tingling.
  • If you are over 65, the maker's page has an FAQ for you. Start with the beginner routines and move up slowly.
  • Warm up lightly before deeper stretches and avoid bouncing.
  • See a professional if stiffness comes with swelling, persistent pain or sudden loss of movement.
