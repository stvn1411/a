What is Breathing for Sleep and how is it meant to work?

Breathing for Sleep is a guided routine rather than a pill. You follow along, lying in bed, with a short sequence of nasal breathing, a gentle sinus massage and a tongue-posture exercise that the author calls a "tongue trick". The goal is to settle your breathing, release tension in the face and jaw, and shift your body towards a calmer, sleep-ready state.

The idea behind it is easy to follow. When the tongue rests low and back in the mouth, and breathing is shallow or through the mouth, the airway can feel narrower and the body can stay a little more on alert. The program teaches you to breathe slowly through the nose and to rest the tongue in a more forward position, both of which many people find relaxing.

The program was created by Zach Zenios, who runs the Critical Bench health and fitness channel. It is sold as a one-time digital purchase, so you can start the same day.

Does a breathing routine like Breathing for Sleep work?

The sales page explains the program through a "sleep nerve" and an "oxygen blockade". We could not verify those specific explanations, so we looked at the broader research on the techniques the program actually uses. It is encouraging, especially for relaxation and sleep quality:

  • In a randomized trial of older adults with insomnia, diaphragmatic (slow, deep) breathing practised for 30 minutes before bed improved sleep-quality measures, in a comparison that also included cognitive-behavioral therapy.[4]
  • A small pilot trial of slow breathing before sleep found some improvement in sleep indices in young adults, although the effects on measured sleep were not large.[5]
  • Pooled analyses of tongue and throat exercises (orofacial myofunctional therapy) reported meaningful reductions in apnea-hypopnea index, with the best results as an add-on to other care. A later Cochrane review judged the evidence to be low certainty.[2][3]

What this means for you: slow nasal breathing and tongue-posture exercises are research-backed, low-risk habits, and Breathing for Sleep packages them into an easy 10-minute guide. The program itself has not had its own clinical trial, so treat it as a relaxing bedtime habit to try for a few weeks, not as a quick fix.

How to get the most out of Breathing for Sleep

  1. Watch the coaching video once in the daytime so you know the steps before you get into bed.
  2. Use the audio version at night so you can close your eyes and follow along.
  3. Be consistent. The author suggests at least 30 days, and slow-breathing studies usually ran for several weeks.
  4. Pair it with the basics: a regular bedtime, a cool dark room and less caffeine late in the day.
  5. Keep a simple note of how long it takes you to settle and how you feel in the morning.

Is Breathing for Sleep safe? Side effects

Gentle breathing and tongue-posture exercises are low risk for most healthy adults, and there is nothing to swallow. If deliberate breathing ever makes you feel light-headed or uncomfortable, ease off and return to your normal breathing.

  • If you have been diagnosed with sleep apnea, keep using your prescribed treatment and talk to your doctor before relying on a breathing routine.
  • Loud snoring with pauses in breathing, gasping at night or heavy daytime sleepiness are reasons to see a doctor, not to self-treat.
  • Educational programs are not reviewed by the FDA as medical treatments.[1]

How Breathing for Sleep compares with other options

  • Melatonin and sleep supplements: these can help some people, but they are products you take nightly. Breathing for Sleep is a skill you keep.
  • Meditation or sleep apps: good options that work on the same relaxation principle. Breathing for Sleep stands out for its focus on breathing and tongue posture, and its low one-time cost.
  • Cognitive-behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I): a well-evidenced approach for long-lasting insomnia, best done with a professional. A breathing routine can sit comfortably beside it.[4]
