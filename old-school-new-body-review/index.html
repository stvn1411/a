What is Old School New Body and how is it meant to work?

Old School New Body (often shortened to OSNB) is a handbook you read and put into action, rather than a pill or a subscription. It was written for adults over 35, though the authors say the same plan suits people in their 20s and 30s as well. The official page says it can be read in about two to three hours.

The idea is simple. Instead of long cardio sessions or hour-long classes, you do a short resistance-training routine built on four exercises, performed in a specific way. The authors call it the F4X, or Focus4, protocol and describe it as about 90 minutes a week in total. Women and men follow the same basic routine, with the weight used being the main difference.

You then choose a phase that matches your goal: F4X Lean for fat loss, F4X Shape for adding a little more lean muscle, and F4X Build for more muscle size. A maintenance routine is included for when you reach the body you want. The handbook describes each phase as the same foundation with small changes to lifting style and eating, which keeps it easy to follow.

It is not a quick fix. The authors are upfront that the plan asks for hard effort in short bursts and some changes to what you eat. If you like clear structure and short sessions, that is a real strength.

Does Old School New Body work?

No study has tested this specific program, but the methods it is built on have good research behind them:

  • In a classic study, ten frail nursing-home residents averaging 90 years old did 8 weeks of high-intensity resistance training. Nine finished, with average strength gains of 174%, a 9% increase in thigh muscle area and a 48% improvement in walking speed.[1] It was small and uncontrolled, but it shows how responsive muscle stays with age.
  • In a one-year randomized trial of 107 older adults with obesity, a diet-plus-exercise group improved physical performance by 21%, compared with 12% for diet alone, 15% for exercise alone and 1% for the control group. The combined group also lost less lean mass and hip bone density than the diet-only group.[2]
  • A meta-analysis of 49 trials with 1,863 participants found that protein supplementation added to resistance training gave small but significant gains in strength and muscle mass, with no extra benefit beyond about 1.6 g of protein per kg of body weight per day.[3]
  • A meta-analysis of nine studies found that strength gains rose with more weekly sets, but lower-volume training still produced meaningful gains.[4]

What this means for you: strength training plus sensible eating is one of the best-supported ways to improve strength, function and body composition in midlife and beyond. Old School New Body packages that approach into a short, structured plan. The program itself has not had its own trial, and results depend on how consistently you follow it, so treat it as a well-grounded plan rather than a promise of a specific result.

How to get the most out of Old School New Body

  1. Read the handbook and Quick Start guide first. The authors stress that the four exercises must be done in a specific way, so don't guess.
  2. Start with the phase that matches your goal. The authors suggest beginning with Lean if fat loss is your aim.
  3. Learn good form before adding weight. Ask a trainer to check your technique if you are new to lifting.
  4. Be consistent. Studies on strength training typically measure changes over 8 to 12 weeks or longer.
  5. Pay attention to protein, water and sleep. These support recovery and are covered in the nutrition plan and bonus guides.
  6. Track your progress with weights lifted, how clothes fit and how you feel, not just the scale.

Is Old School New Body safe?

Resistance training is generally safe for healthy adults when done with good form and gradual progression, and the authors say the plan is designed with older trainees in mind. Muscle soreness is common when you start. In the one-year trial we cited, a small number of exercise-related musculoskeletal injuries were reported.[2]

  • Check with your doctor before starting any new exercise or eating plan if you have a heart condition, high blood pressure, diabetes, joint or back problems, or take prescription medicine.
  • Warm up, use weights you can control, and stop if you feel sharp pain, dizziness or chest discomfort.
  • The program is a handbook, not a supplement, so it does not need FDA approval, but its health claims are the authors' own and results vary from person to person.
  • See a doctor promptly for chest pain, severe shortness of breath, fainting or persistent joint pain.

How Old School New Body compares with other options

  • Gym classes and cardio plans: these are enjoyable and good for heart health. Old School New Body focuses on strength instead, which the research above supports for muscle and function.
  • Personal trainers: a trainer gives one-to-one feedback on form, but costs much more. This handbook costs a small fraction of that and can be combined with a trainer's form check.
  • Weight-loss supplements: supplements such as our reviews of 24 BURN and Leptozan take a different, capsule-based approach. A strength plan like this one can sit alongside a healthy routine, and it needs no capsules.
